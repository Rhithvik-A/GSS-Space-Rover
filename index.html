◇ ENCELADUS PROFILE

Key Features

Everything we know about Saturn's most intriguing moon.

🪐
ORBITS SATURN
Enceladus orbits within Saturn's vast ring system, specifically within the tenuous E ring, which is actually generated by its own plume activity. It completes one orbit every 32.9 hours at a distance of about 238,000 km from Saturn. The gravitational tug from Saturn and the moon Dione creates tidal heating that powers its geological activity.
📏
COMPACT SIZE
With a diameter of about 504 km, Enceladus is only about 1/25th the size of Earth. It's small enough to fit within the borders of the UK or the US state of Arizona. Despite its tiny size, it packs more geological excitement than many larger worlds — proving that size doesn't determine scientific importance.
❄️
ICY SURFACE
The surface is almost entirely covered in fresh, clean ice, making Enceladus one of the most reflective objects in the Solar System (albedo ~0.99). The south polar region features dramatic "tiger stripe" fractures — four parallel rifts about 130 km long — that are the source of the moon's famous plumes.
🌊
HIDDEN OCEAN
Beneath roughly 20–25 km of ice, Cassini data strongly suggests a global liquid-water ocean. The ocean is kept warm by tidal heating from Saturn's gravity. Measurements of the moon's wobble (libration) indicate the ice shell floats on this ocean rather than being frozen to the rocky core — meaning the entire moon has a liquid layer.
💨
WATER PLUMES
Enceladus shoots massive jets of water vapor, ice particles, and organic compounds hundreds of kilometers into space from cracks near its south pole. These plumes were first discovered by the Cassini spacecraft in 2005. They eject approximately 200 kg of material per second and are the primary source of Saturn's E ring.
🧪
LIFE INGREDIENTS
Cassini detected complex organic molecules, molecular hydrogen (H₂), carbon dioxide, and silica nanoparticles in the plumes. The hydrogen suggests hydrothermal activity on the ocean floor — similar to deep-sea vents on Earth where life thrives. Enceladus has liquid water, energy, and chemistry — all ingredients for potential life.
◇ EXPLORATION HISTORY

Enceladus Discovery Timeline

1789
William Herschel discovers Enceladus using his 1.2-meter telescope — one of the first moons found beyond Jupiter.
1981
Voyager 2 flyby reveals a surprisingly smooth, young surface — hinting at geological activity.
2005
Cassini discovers dramatic water-ice plumes erupting from the south polar "tiger stripes" — a landmark moment.
2008 – 2015
Multiple Cassini flybys detect organic molecules, molecular hydrogen, and confirm a global subsurface ocean.
2017
Scientists confirm Enceladus has all known requirements for life: liquid water, energy source, and organic chemistry.
2026 — NOW
